VR podracer · twin printed throttles · design spec v0.1 · 16 Oct 2026

Phaethon

A seated VR podracer flown with two physical throttle levers, one per engine. Benchmarked against Star Wars: Galactic Racer (Fuse Games, 6 Oct 2026), and built around the one thing a gamepad game can't do: put your hands on the engines.

This is the flight model from §3, seen from above. Equal levers fly straight. Lead with one lever to turn. Squeeze one brake to pivot. Push both levers past the white line for the afterburner, or arm the ramjet first with R. An Xbox pad works too: the sticks become the levers and the triggers become the brakes. When the printed levers exist, they plug into this same page.

Tune the craft

§1

A podracer is a chariot

The Boonta Eve race is staged after the chariot race in Ben-Hur, and the hardware matches. The two engines are the horses, the cables are the traces, and the pod is the chariot. A car steers with its front wheels. A chariot steers by making one horse work harder than the other. So the core verb of this game is differential thrust, and you feel it in two hands.

That decides almost everything else. The input is two levers, not a wheel. The physics is three bodies on a tether, not one rigid car. The camera is your seat, because the pod is the thing being dragged. The name fits too: Phaethon borrowed his father's sun chariot, couldn't hold the horses, and was struck down by Zeus. Consider it a difficulty setting.

§2

Benchmark: Galactic Racer

Star Wars: Galactic Racer launched on 6 October 2026 from Fuse Games, a studio founded by ex-Criterion developers. It's built on Unreal Engine 5 and sits at 84–88 on Metacritic. It is the bar for how a Star Wars racer should feel at speed. It is also a flat-screen gamepad game whose podracer is the least stable craft and lives mostly in Arcade mode. That leaves a gap.

The craft model

Three bodies and two kinds of joint. Steering, sway and drift fall out of the model instead of being scripted. The lab at the top runs exactly this model in 2D. The 3D version adds a hover spring per body and nothing else.

What the lab found

All four results came from headless runs of the lab's physics core at 480 Hz.

Corner cables go unstable near 715 km/h

With one cable on each front corner of the pod, its yaw grows exponentially at top speed until one cable goes slack and the pod hangs off the other. That's a stable but lopsided state, and the craft spirals at about 7°/s. Moving both cables onto a nose ring fixed it. Over 60 s at 716 km/h the drift measured 0.00°/s, and a 25 m/s sideways kick damped out completely, with peak sway of 6°.

Per-engine tension is trailer sway

If each cable pulls only its own engine, a feedback loop forms. The pod drifts right, the left cable goes taut, the left engine lags, the craft turns left, and the pod is flung further right. Result: a 16°/s spiral at 683 km/h. Sending 80% of the tension through a bridle on the binder removes the loop. The remaining 20% lets sway still tug at the craft.

Steering is proportional

At about 700 km/h, a 25% lever split turns at about 6°/s and a 50% split at about 12°/s. A full split turns at 46°/s, but the craft slides and bleeds down to about 390 km/h. One air brake alone turns at about 20°/s. Small moves give small turns, so the levers can be precise.

Boost numbers land near the benchmark

Cruise at full lever is 716 km/h. The afterburner reaches 805 km/h on a 3.6 s battery. The ramjet reaches 934 km/h and flames out after 4.2 s from cold. A 9.2 km test track takes about 80 s per lap on a crude autopilot.

Startup sequence

  1. BINDER on. An arc snaps between the engines and the cockpit picks up a hum.
  2. LEFT ignition. The left engine coughs, catches and spools up over about 2 s. Audio is panned left.
  3. RIGHT ignition. The same on the right. Now you can hear both engines as separate machines.
  4. Levers to idle. The start lights won't run until both levers sit in the idle notch, like the interlock on a real engine.
  5. Lights. Go.

This is the moment Galactic Racer can't touch. Thirty seconds of flipping real switches before every race does more for presence than any shader.

§5

Heat, damage, repair

  • Heat is per engine. It rises above 60% lever and much faster in the afterburner or ramjet. Cooling grows with airspeed. Track zones change it, matching the benchmark: ice doubles cooling, while lava and exhaust vents add heat.
  • Flameout at 100%. That engine dies, and the craft yaws hard toward the dead side. The instinctive save is to pull the other lever back. The drama is native to the levers, with no button prompt involved. Reignite works once the engine is below 60%.
  • Ramjet past the limit. In the full game, holding the ramjet past 115% heat blows the engine and ends the race, which matches the benchmark. The lab stops at flameout.
  • Damage costs thrust. A wall strike harder than 6 m/s damages whichever body hit. A damaged engine delivers 45–100% of its thrust depending on health, and sputters below 50%. To fly straight you have to hold a lever split. You feel the damage in your hands rather than reading it off a gauge.
  • Binder snap. If the engines are forced more than 3 m off their spacing, the binder breaks and the race is over. Hitting a rival's binder beam becomes the takedown move in version 2.
§6

VR comfort at 900 km/h

Steady forward speed is cheap in VR. Unexpected rotation is what makes people sick. The design works on the rotation side.

  • The pod is the reference frame. You sit 1:1 in a solid cockpit that never moves relative to your body. Sim racers ride high speeds in VR on exactly this principle.
  • The tether filters your head motion. The engines yaw first and the pod follows a beat later through the cables. You see a turn start out in front of you before your viewpoint rotates. Whether that measurably helps comfort is an open question until someone wears it.
  • You cause the motion. Drivers get carsick less than passengers, and two physical levers put you firmly in the driver's role.
  • Rules: never change the field of view. No camera cuts or slow-motion replays during a race; a crash fades to black and respawns you. Pod roll is clamped to ±8° on screen, with a full horizon lock as an option. An optional vignette appears when yaw passes 40°/s. Frame rate never drops below 90 Hz (11.1 ms per frame), with 120 Hz if the budget allows. Speed comes across through ground detail, audio and wind, never through FOV.
  • Virtual levers line up with the real ones. Calibration is one step: rest a Quest controller on each grip and press a button. It's the same anchor trick Euterpe uses on the piano. Your hand meets the lever exactly where you see it.
§7

The printed levers

Two lever units and a switch dash on one baseplate, connected by one USB cable. The whole rig shows up as a standard HID game controller with four axes and eight buttons, so it needs no drivers. Godot reads it, and so does this page through the Gamepad API.

Mechanism, per lever

  • Quadrant: two printed cheeks with 608ZZ bearings pressed in, an 8 mm stainless or aluminium D-shaft, and 60° of throw. Two bumper posts across the top double as the hard stops. They sit exactly where the hub's socket lands at −2° and 62°.
  • Arm: a 16 mm aluminium tube, 189 mm long, clamped into a printed hub and grip. The grip centre sits about 260 mm from the pivot. This changes the original plan: a printed arm is the weakest part in bending and doesn't fit a 220 bed, and the tube carries the grip wiring inside it.
  • Friction lock: a felt-faced shoe pressed against the hub rim by an M5 thumbscrew, so the lever holds where you leave it, the way a real throttle quadrant does.
  • Detents (M4): the same hub rim gets the profile: an idle notch at 0%, a step at the 88% gate, and a spring ramp past it. A spring-loaded follower replaces the shoe. Reprinting the hub retunes the feel without touching firmware.
  • Grip: printed in two halves, chunky and pod-style. The brake paddle pivots on an M3 shoulder bolt with a return spring, and the thumb button sits on top.
  • Base: 18 mm plywood. The levers sit in slots so their spacing adjusts; start at about 380 mm centre to centre. The base clamps to a desk or bolts to a chair rig.

  • Firmware: the Arduino-Pico core's USB Joystick library, or TinyUSB directly. Axes report idle to maximum across the full range, which the Gamepad API reads as −1 to 1. Holding both thumb buttons for 3 s captures each axis's minimum and maximum and saves them to flash.
  • Gotcha: tie the AS5600's DIR pin to ground. Left floating, the direction can flip at random.
  • Wiring: run the grip wires down a channel inside the arm. Where they leave near the pivot, use a grommet and a service loop, so the 60° swing never flexes wire over a hard edge.

Printing

  • PETG for the cheeks, arm and cam. Four walls, 35% gyroid infill.
  • Print the arm on its side so the layers run along it. Bending across layer lines is how a lever snaps.
  • Use brass heat-set inserts (M3/M4) anywhere you'll unscrew more than twice.
  • Every part fits a 220×220 mm bed. The arm splits at a dovetail if it needs to.
  • Finish in the used-universe style: grey primer, silver base coat, chipped paint over it, an oil wash in the recesses, and hazard stripes on the gate.

§9

Roadmap and gates

Each milestone ships only when its gate passes on the real thing.

  1. Feel lab. passed The straight line is stable at 716 km/h, steering is proportional, laps complete, and nothing goes NaN. Verified headless; the browser render was checked before publishing.
  2. Godot flat-screen port. passed A recorded 4,912-frame input log, replayed through both sims, gives 81.8229 s in each: zero error at four decimals over 39,000 steps. The RNG and the track match exactly. A chase-cam view runs on keyboard, gamepad or the levers.
  3. VR seat. gate A 10-minute session holds 90 Hz on a frame-timing log, and you rate comfort "mild" or better on a 0–3 scale. This one needs you in the headset.
  4. One lever. gate One lever, no detents yet. It shows up in Windows game controllers and in this page's input readout, and jitters no more than ±2 counts at rest. Ready to print: 10 parametric parts, all inside a 220 bed. The swing is clash-tested free from −1.5° to 61.5°, with real overlap at −4° and 64° proving the stops. The firmware compiles to a 64 KB UF2 with a built-in jitter test.
  5. Full cockpit. gate The startup sequence and a clean VR lap, using only the physical controls.
  6. Race. gate Three AI, a ghost, and one canyon track with an ice zone and a lava zone. Your lap times improve across 5 sessions, which proves it's learnable.

Version 2 adds shunt combat, binder takedowns and a second track. One candidate for that track is your own drone-scanned terrain: race podracers through the back forty.

§10

Assumptions to veto

  • PCVR on Quest 3, seated. Taken from your existing VR setup notes.
  • Godot 4 over Unreal. Galactic Racer uses UE5, but this game doesn't need that renderer. It needs fast iteration and the VR pipeline you already have.
  • Printer. The model is unknown, so the parts assume a 220×220 bed and PETG.
  • Personal project. If it ever goes public, the Star Wars names come off. The mechanics are generic.
  • Single-player first. No roguelite, no hub on foot.
